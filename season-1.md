# Edge of History: Season 1 Episode Guide

**The direction for Season 1** is deep-cut stories from the 20th century's underground: counterculture, power, secrets, cults and the people who quietly built the modern world. These are stories everyone has half-heard of, about people almost nobody can actually explain. Each one has a "wait, *what*?" fact at its centre, and each sits close to a big, searchable name (Oppenheimer, Bitcoin, Jonestown) so new listeners can find it.

**The thread through the season:** these episodes keep running into each other. The CIA's LSD programme connects Kesey (Ep 1) to Operation Midnight Climax (Ep 8). Roy Cohn (Ep 2) turns up again as Studio 54's lawyer. "Drinking the Kool-Aid" connects the Acid Tests (Ep 1) to Jonestown (Ep 4), and the internet's builders (Ep 3) connect to Bitcoin (Ep 6) and the phone phreaks (Ep 10). Nora keeps a corkboard with red string and calls back to it, and fans will start drawing the map themselves.

**Launch trio:** Episodes 1 to 3 go out together. They cover three different worlds (the counterculture, political power and technology), so a new listener can find their way in through whichever one hooks them.

**Moved out of Season 1:** the Mona Lisa, Titanic and Napoleon scripts are kept in `archive/` as possible bonus episodes. They're too mainstream to define the show.

## Episode 1: The CIA Paid for Ken Kesey's First Acid Trip

- **Hook:** Around 1959, a broke Stanford writing student signed up for paid drug trials at the Menlo Park VA hospital. The research belonged to a web of government-funded psychedelic studies, widely linked to the CIA's MKUltra programme (some sources point to Army funding instead, and Cal says so). The government wanted to know whether LSD could be a weapon. What it got was Ken Kesey, *One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest* and the Sixties.
- **Beats:** (1) the hospital, the night shifts and the novel. (2) The bus *Further*, a 1939 International Harvester school bus painted in Day-Glo, driven across America in 1964 by Neal Cassady, who had been "Dean Moriarty" in *On the Road*. That's the handoff from the Beats to the hippies, in one driver's seat. They go to the World's Fair, Kesey writes that it is "not a cool place," and they visit Timothy Leary's people at Millbrook, who find them too much. (3) The Acid Tests of 1965–66, with the Grateful Dead as house band and Owsley Stanley, the LSD chemist who also built the Dead's sound system. (4) August 1965: Hunter S. Thompson brings the Hells Angels to Kesey's party at La Honda, Allen Ginsberg chants at them, and the Angels take acid and *don't* kill anyone. (5) Kesey fakes his own suicide to flee a marijuana charge to Mexico.
- **Citation Needed:** "The CIA gave Kesey LSD" is IT'S COMPLICATED. The trials and the payments were real, the funding chain is disputed, and he didn't know who was behind it. "The Acid Tests spiked the Kool-Aid" is TRUE at the later Watts Acid Test, where people didn't know the Kool-Aid was dosed.
- **Who profited?** Tom Wolfe (*The Electric Kool-Aid Acid Test*, 1968), the Grateful Dead, and, Nora argues, the CIA, which accidentally funded its own counterculture.
- **The Footnote:** The bus. It rotted for years in a swamp on Kesey's farm in Oregon. The Smithsonian asked for it and Kesey refused. Then the family restored it.
- **Respect to…** the bus driver: Neal Cassady.

## Episode 2: Roy Cohn Hunted Gay Men for the Government. He Was One.

- **Hook:** At 23 he helped send the Rosenbergs to the electric chair. At 26 he was Joe McCarthy's chief counsel, helping purge gay men from the federal government during the "Lavender Scare." He was gay himself, and he spent his nights in a Manhattan where queer life ran through Mafia-owned bars, society clubs and unspoken rules.
- **Beats:** (1) Cohn's secret contact with the judge during the Rosenberg trial. (2) The postwar New York underground: the Stork Club, mob-run gay bars, entrapment raids, and how everyone knew and nobody said. (3) G. David Schine, Cohn's handsome friend, and Cohn's campaign to get the Army to give him special treatment, which triggered the Army–McCarthy hearings and "Have you no sense of decency?" (4) His later life as fixer and lawyer to mobsters, Studio 54 and power brokers, his disbarment, and his 1986 death from AIDS, which he insisted was liver cancer.
- **Register:** The comedy is aimed at power and hypocrisy, never at the victims of the purges. Thousands of people lost their careers.
- **The Footnote:** The AIDS Memorial Quilt has a panel for Roy Cohn. It reads, "Bully. Coward. Victim."
- **To verify when scripting:** the details of the ex parte contact with Judge Kaufman, and the quilt panel wording.

## Episode 3: In 1998, One Man Quietly Took Over the Internet. Then He Gave It Back.

- **Hook:** On 28 January 1998, Jon Postel, a bearded, sandal-wearing academic at USC who personally kept the internet's master list of addresses, emailed most of the root-server operators and asked them to point to *his* machine instead of the government-contracted one. Most of them did. For a few days, the internet's address book answered to one man's email. Then the White House called.
- **Beats:** (1) Elizabeth "Jake" Feinler, who ran the ARPANET's Network Information Center for nearly two decades, kept the master directory of every host on the network, and whose team helped settle the naming scheme behind .com, .edu and .gov. (2) Bob Taylor, who reportedly got the ARPANET funded in a twenty-minute meeting because he was sick of having three computer terminals in his office. (3) Postel, the "god of the internet," who edited the RFCs and ran address assignments from his office for decades. (4) The 1998 "test," the backlash, and the creation of ICANN. Postel died that October, before ICANN was finished.
- **Respect to…** the sysadmin.
- **The Footnote:** Jake Feinler's directory began as a printed book, and people mailed in their updates.

## Episode 4: Jim Jones Had City Hall on Speed Dial

- **Hook:** Before Jonestown, Jim Jones was a respected San Francisco power broker. He could deliver busloads of volunteers and voters, and he was praised by people who should have known better. The mayor gave him a city post running the Housing Authority. "Drinking the Kool-Aid" is a phrase about the victims, and it's wrong: the drink was mostly Flavor Aid.
- **Register:** SERIOUS. 918 people died in Guyana on 18 November 1978, about 300 of them children. The banter stops sooner and runs quieter. The jokes go only at the enablers and the institutions. The bet is replaced by a sincere listener note.
- **Beats:** the integrated church that drew idealists, the political machine, the defectors and the press, Congressman Leo Ryan's trip (Jackie Speier was shot and survived, and later served in Congress herself), and the final tape.
- **The Footnote:** The survivors, the people who walked out into the jungle that morning, and what they say about the phrase "drink the Kool-Aid."
- **Season link:** the Kool-Aid of the Acid Tests to the Flavor Aid of Jonestown. Cal handles it carefully.

## Episode 5: The Rocket Scientist Who Tried to Summon a Goddess (With L. Ron Hubbard)

- **Hook:** Jack Parsons helped found what became NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory. He was also a devoted occultist in Aleister Crowley's movement. In 1946 he and a charming navy veteran named L. Ron Hubbard performed rituals in the Mojave to summon a goddess. Then Hubbard ran off with Parsons's girlfriend and his money, and later founded Scientology.
- **Beats:** the "Suicide Squad" of rocket enthusiasts at Caltech, the mansion on Pasadena's "Millionaire's Row" full of bohemians, the Babalon Working, the yacht scam, and Parsons's death in an explosion in 1952.
- **The Footnote:** There's a crater on the far side of the Moon named after him.

## Episode 6: The Man Who Got the First Bitcoin Is Frozen in Arizona

- **Hook:** In January 2009, Satoshi Nakamoto sent ten bitcoins to Hal Finney, a cypherpunk programmer who'd downloaded the software on day one. Finney was later diagnosed with ALS, kept coding with eye-tracking software, died in 2014, and was cryopreserved at the Alcor facility in Scottsdale.
- **Beats:** the cypherpunk mailing list, the white paper, the pizza bought for 10,000 BTC on 22 May 2010, Satoshi's goodbye in 2011, and the hunt for Satoshi, including the man a UK court ruled in 2024 is *not* Satoshi.
- **Register:** light and nerdy, landing in something moving.

## Episode 7: Oppenheimer's Secret City Didn't Exist. Its Neighbours Did.

- **Hook:** Los Alamos officially didn't exist, and babies born there had "P.O. Box 1663" on their birth certificates. When the Trinity test went off in July 1945, the families living downwind in New Mexico's Tularosa Basin were never warned or evacuated, and for decades they were left out of federal compensation.
- **Beats:** the town built in secret, the wives who ran it, the spies inside it, the test, and the downwinders' long fight to be heard.
- **To verify when scripting:** the current status of compensation under the Radiation Exposure Compensation Act.

## Episode 8: The CIA Ran a Brothel in San Francisco. It Was Called Midnight Climax.

- **Hook:** In the 1950s and '60s, under MKUltra, the CIA ran apartments in San Francisco and New York where sex workers dosed unsuspecting men with LSD while agents watched from behind two-way mirrors. The man in charge was a narcotics agent named George Hunter White.
- **Register:** absurd and horrifying in equal measure. The victims never consented, and the episode never forgets that.
- **Season link:** it's the same programme that, at the edges, paid Ken Kesey.

## Episode 9: Waco: 51 Days

- **Hook:** On 28 February 1993, federal agents raided the Branch Davidian compound outside Waco. A 51-day standoff followed, and it ended in fire on 19 April. Nora's angle is the negotiation tapes. Cal's is how many different stories America has told about Waco since.
- **Register:** SERIOUS, handled like Episode 4. Some 76 Branch Davidians died on the last day, including more than 20 children, as well as four ATF agents in the original raid.

## Episode 10 (Finale): The Blind Kid Who Could Whistle Into the Phone Network

- **Hook:** Joe Engressia was born blind and had perfect pitch. As a child he found he could whistle the 2600 Hz tone that controlled AT&T's long-distance network. He inspired a generation of "phone phreaks," including two young Californians named Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak, who sold illegal "blue boxes" before they built Apple.
- **The payoff:** Cal finally wins a bet and the "Nora Was Wrong" tally goes to one.
